Case file

Nine students, one bacterium. Who else is carrying it — and who passed it to whom?

Sue collapsed during class on Monday morning. At the hospital a doctor drew a little of the fluid from around her spinal cord, and the lab found Neisseria meningitidis in it — the bacterium behind bacterial meningitis. It travels easily between people who share a room, a meal or a practice session, and it can turn dangerous within a day. So Sue and the eight classmates she had spent the most time with were all sampled that same afternoon. Those nine samples are what your class has to work through.

An ELISA — an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay — asks two questions of every sample. Is the bacterium's antigen in there at all? That answer is the diagnosis. And how much of it is in there? That one is worth more than it looks, because antigen keeps building up from the moment a person is infected: whoever is carrying the most has been carrying it the longest. Put the nine results in order and you are looking at the route the infection took through the class.

Your two samples

Your instructor gives each group two of the nine samples. Choose the two you were handed; the other seven reach you at the end, when the rest of the class reports back.

    Module 1 · at the bench

    1. Label both 12-well strips, 1–12 and 13–24.
    2. Dilution buffer, 50 µL into wells 2–12.
    3. Antigen, 100 µL into well 1.
    4. Serial dilution — 50 µL along the strip, mixing five times at each well.
    5. Controls and patients, 50 µL into three wells each.
    6. Incubate, then wash twice.
    7. Primary antibody → incubate → wash. Secondary → incubate → wash.
    8. TMB substrate, develop, then stop solution.

    Drag a tool and rest its tip over a target for a moment to act — the tip box to fit a tip, a tube to draw a dose, a well to dispense. Clicking works too. Three tools come out of the drawer: the micropipette, the transfer pipet for wash buffer, and a fine-tipped marker.

    Or switch on Hand control in the masthead and use your own hand in front of the webcam. With a real micropipette, choose Calibrate grip and capture your resting grip, pressed plunger and pressed tip ejector. Keep your thumb and wrist in view. During each capture, gently turn through your usual working angles. Then rest your grip over the on-screen micropipette to pick it up and aim with the ring at its tip. Press and release the plunger to aspirate, press to dispense, and press the tip ejector over waste to remove the tip. Use Set down in the camera panel to park the virtual pipette. Choose Free-hand gestures to use an empty hand instead; close your fingers to pick up a tool and open them to place it.

    The strips and the paper towels can be slid up or down the bench by dragging their captions — or with the arrow keys, from the ⇕ handle at the end of each one. Under the camera that is worth doing before you start: put the wells at the height your own hand sits at comfortably, so your arm never has to leave the picture to reach them. The camera panel moves the same way — drag it by its own header if it is sitting on something you are reading. With an empty hand, pinch over a caption and keep pinching to move it; separate your fingers to place it. A quick pinch elsewhere clicks. If tracking pauses, hold your hand in view again — the tool stays still while the camera finds it.

    Module 2 · the read

    1. Check the controls — wells 13–15 must be strongly coloured, 16–18 clear.
    2. Diagnose each patient positive or negative.
    3. Quantify — match each patient well against the standard strip.
    4. Share with the rest of the class and fill Table C.
    5. Trace the chain of infection from the antigen loads.

    Module 2 can be opened on its own. If you have not run the assay, a prepared plate is put on the light box for you.

    ⚠ Gloves and goggles at all times. Never mouth-pipette a reagent.

Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2026 · Henri B. Kagan & Kenso Soai

How one handhand wins

Many molecules come in two mirror-image forms, and life builds its proteins from only one. Kagan showed that a catalyst can make a product more one-handed than itself. Soai built a reaction that turns a tiny excess of one form into an almost pure sample. Six steps, about 3½ minutes.

Prize citation: “for the discovery of non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis”

You can’t match them. The best any rotation achieves is 2 of 4 groups. A molecule that can’t be stacked onto its mirror image is chiral, like your hands. The two forms are enantiomers.

To name each hand, tap Look down C–H. Rank the groups (NH₂ > COOH > CH₃ > H), point the lowest one, H, away from you, and trace 1 → 2 → 3. Counterclockwise is S; clockwise is R.

  • Twins in most ways. Same boiling point, melting point and solubility in non-chiral surroundings. They rotate polarized light by equal amounts in opposite directions.
  • Told apart by chiral things. Your nose has chiral receptors: (R)-carvone smells like spearmint, (S)-carvone like caraway.
  • Life is one-handed. Your proteins use only L-amino acids. Ordinary lab reactions with no chiral influence give a 50:50 (racemic) mix.

Kagan, 1986: catalysts built from pairs

~40 s

In Kagan’s model, a catalyst is a metal atom holding two chiral pieces called ligands. Chemists assumed product ee was proportional to ligand ee: halve the ligand’s ee, halve the product’s. Kagan showed this can fail.

Each dot pair on the right is one catalyst. A mixed pair (one of each hand) is not chiral overall, so it makes a 50:50 product.

2003: one hand from no bias

~30 s

Now start with no chiral catalyst. The first product molecules form at random, like coin flips, so the count is almost never exactly 50:50. Can the reaction amplify that noise? Run some flasks.

Your simulated flasksNo flasks yet
Soai’s 37 real flasks (2003)19 S · 18 R · ee 15–91%

Toy model: 1,000 random first molecules, then each hand catalyzes its own formation at a rate second-order in its own amount (mixed pairs inactive), 98% selective. It mimics the behavior. The real mechanism is still debated.

Each flask picks a hand at random, and most end up strongly enriched in it. Chemists call this absolute asymmetric synthesis: a one-handed product with no chiral input. Add a tiny chiral nudge, such as a quartz crystal or circularly polarized light, and the nudge decides which hand wins.

Why a Nobel?

~20 s

For medicine makers

Many drugs are made as a single hand, because the mirror form can be inactive or harmful. Chemists now use non-linear effects to work out how catalysts work and to get purer products from impure ingredients. At Merck, a reagent made from α‑pinene that was only 70% optically pure gave a product with 95% ee.

For the origin of life

Frank ended his 1953 paper: “A laboratory demonstration may not be impossible.” Soai delivered it. The Nobel committee calls it an important proof of concept, but not relevant to how life’s water-based chemistry became one-handed (its zinc reagent is destroyed by water). How life chose its hand is still an open question.

AP Chemistry connections

  • Unit 2 (VSEPR, hybridization): tetrahedral sp³ carbon vs. trigonal planar sp² C=O. A chiral center forms when a group adds to one face of a planar carbonyl.
  • Unit 3 (IMFs, properties): enantiomers have identical IMFs, so identical boiling points and solubilities in non-chiral settings.
  • Unit 5 (kinetics): catalysts open a lower-Ea path and are regenerated; parallel catalyst species each contribute rate = k[species]; autocatalysis, where rate rises as product (the catalyst) accumulates.

Misconceptions to watch

  • “L means left-handed.” D/L is a naming convention, not a literal direction, and it doesn’t predict which way light rotates. L-alanine is (S); L-cysteine is (R).
  • “All amino acids are chiral.” Glycine (two H’s) is not. The glycine toggle in tab 1 shows this.
  • “Enantiomers have identical properties, period.” Only in non-chiral settings. They rotate plane-polarized light oppositely and behave differently toward chiral receptors, catalysts and solvents.
  • “Catalysts are never made in a reaction.” A catalyst isn’t consumed. In autocatalysis the amount grows because the product is the catalyst.
  • “Soai solved the origin of life.” The committee calls it a proof of concept that is not relevant to homochirality in water-based systems; Frank’s model is one of several proposed explanations.
  • “ee = % of the major form.” 90:10 is 80% ee, not 90%.

Data and model notes

  • Kagan’s 1986 paper reported negative non-linear effects for a sulfide oxidation and the proline-catalyzed Hajos–Parrish reaction, and a positive one for the Sharpless epoxidation of geraniol. Later work (Hoang, Bahmanyar, Houk & List, 2003) found the Hajos–Parrish reaction to be linear.
  • Tab 3 uses Kagan’s ML₂ picture with random pairing and a perfectly selective matched catalyst. Real systems often favor the mixed complex thermodynamically, which strengthens the effect (Noyori’s 15% → 98% example).
  • Soai 1990: catalyst at 86% ee gave product at only 35% ee (pyridine version). Soai 1995: the Nature abstract describes a run starting at 2% ee; the committee’s scientific background tabulates 5% → 55% → 87% → 90% plateau, which this page uses. “Plain copying” is the committee’s own hypothetical.
  • Tab 5’s toy model is second-order homochiral autocatalysis (equivalent to inactive mixed pairs). Daniel Singleton independently showed absolute asymmetric synthesis in the Soai reaction (2002). Several mechanisms have been proposed (Blackmond; Gridnev & Brown; Denmark 2020; Trapp 2020) and are still debated.
  • Merck example: the α-pinene was used to make the reducing agent (chlorodiisopinocampheylborane), used in stoichiometric amounts.
